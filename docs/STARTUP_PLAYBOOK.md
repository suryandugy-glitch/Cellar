# Startup playbook — Cellar

Prepared for Suryandu Ganguly. Treat this as a practical validation plan alongside college work. The Java product is now a demonstrable local beta; a company and a sustainable business still depend on customer evidence, support and distribution.

## 1. What to sell first

**Offer:** “We help your agency catch export changes that break client imports before release, with a report naming the affected workflow and owner.”

Start with one export, up to three consumers, one repository and a 30-day assisted pilot. The deliverable is a working CI check and an agreed migration process. Do not promise complete production correctness or an enterprise SaaS platform.

Target agencies supporting recurring order, inventory, invoice, fulfillment or finance CSV imports. Agricultural software agencies may be a useful discovery channel given your domain background, but validate their actual export pain before restricting the market to agriculture.

## 2. Differentiation to test

| Alternative | Why customers use it | Cellar hypothesis to test |
|---|---|---|
| Manual spreadsheet review | No setup, familiar tools | Repeatable CI findings save release and support time |
| Custom assertions/scripts | Cheap and already tailored | Named consumers, ownership and shared report formats reduce maintenance |
| General data validators | Rich schema/value validation | Workflow-specific impact makes change coordination clearer |
| Contract/schema drift tools | Track structure and changes | Consumer-level migration ownership provides additional value |

Do not claim “first”, “unique”, “no competition” or guaranteed savings. The attached plan names SchemaGlow and Evidence Gate as research leads; their current capabilities have not been independently evaluated in this package. Interview buyers about tools they actually use and perform a current comparison before publishing competitive claims.

## 3. Discovery: first ten conversations

Use alumni, developer communities, agency contacts and integration maintainers. Send messages yourself or explicitly authorize outreach; this project performs none.

Ask about a concrete recent event:

1. What export changed, and which importer broke?
2. Who discovered it, and how long did repair take?
3. What did the incident cost in team time, client dissatisfaction or delayed work?
4. Where are consumer requirements documented today?
5. How often does this happen, and what catches it now?
6. Can CI generate a representative synthetic export?
7. Who owns rollout and who approves spending?
8. Would you install on one repository by a specific date?

Avoid showing the demo before understanding the incident. Record approximate costs as estimates from the customer, not audited savings. Do not request private production records; use redacted schemas and synthetic examples.

## 4. Pilot package

**Included:** discovery call, contract mapping, one CI integration, one reproduced break, weekly review, written closeout. Define support hours and response expectations. Do not promise 24/7 support while operating as a student founder.

**Acceptance:** pilot owner can explain each contract; real exporter fixture passes; agreed rename or value error fails the expected consumer; advisory findings reviewed; branch enforcement enabled only when accepted; rollback instructions documented.

**Exit options:** paid extension to another export/repository; fix a measurable adoption obstacle; stop if value is not demonstrated. Document the reason without overstating traction.

## 5. Pricing experiments, not established prices

| Experiment | Illustrative hypothesis | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Free local core | CLI/desktop remain MIT | Developers install and maintain meaningful contracts |
| Assisted Indian agency pilot | ₹3,000–₹8,000 once for one repository/30 days | Willingness to pay and manageable onboarding effort |
| Ongoing agency support | ₹1,500–₹4,000/month for agreed small scope | Recurring value, support load, renewal |
| Future hosted collaboration | Price only after registry/approval demand | Multiple active repositories and a buyer for team features |

These are interview price points and internal planning figures, not market research or promises. The original plan also suggested testing US$49/month for up to three repositories; test only one coherent offer per segment. Do not convert currencies or treat either number as validated.

MIT allows others to use, modify and redistribute the code. Do not attempt to sell exclusivity over the existing MIT code. Potential paid value is setup, support and future hosted coordination. Review third-party contributions and ownership before changing licensing on future components.

## 6. Illustrative unit economics

Suppose a team pays ₹2,500/month. Allocate ₹300 to shared infrastructure/tools and value two hours of support at an internal planning rate of ₹500/hour. Contribution before acquisition, tax and founder overhead is ₹1,200/month (48%). If support rises to five hours, contribution becomes −₹300: growth would make the problem worse.

The calculation is an example, not a margin forecast. Track actual support minutes, onboarding cost, customer retention, refunds and collection time. Separate cash expenses from unpaid founder time. Measure customer value before building billing automation.

## 7. Ninety-day plan

| Period | Work | Deliverable / decision |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–14 | Run demos, do ten interviews, verify native Windows behavior, refine contracts | Incident notes and two willing pilot owners |
| Days 15–30 | Install with actual exporters, start advisory, reproduce break | Two functioning real-repository pilots |
| Days 31–45 | Tune false alarms, document ownership, agree merge enforcement | Retention and noise evidence; paid decision |
| Days 46–60 | Improve the most common adoption blocker, package releases, measure support | Repeatable onboarding under target time |
| Days 61–75 | If demand supports it, prototype registry/approval workflow design | Validated paid collaboration requirement |
| Days 76–90 | Review renewal/expansion and economics; choose cloud investment or local focus | Explicit continue/adjust/pause decision |

Allow 6–8 hours/week initially: 3 hours engineering/QA, 2 hours customer conversations/onboarding, 1 hour documentation and 1–2 hours follow-up/buffer. Reduce scope when examinations conflict; agree support limits with pilots.

## 8. Roles before hiring

- Founder/product engineer: Java core, onboarding, demos, interviews and roadmap.
- Reviewer/test partner: independent synthetic fixtures, Windows/macOS testing and bug reproduction.
- Optional UI contributor: accessibility, packaging and desktop polish after workflow validation.
- Professional advisors as needed: ownership, contracts, privacy obligations, invoicing and accounting.

These are roles; the first stage does not require four employees. Use collaboration agreements and clear repository access when others contribute.

## 9. Business preparation checklist

Prepare a product name/domain availability check, customer discovery log, short demo, README, license notices, support policy, pilot scope, pricing hypothesis, issue tracker, roadmap and release checklist. Keep source ownership/contributor records and access recovery procedures. Before charging or handling customer data, get jurisdiction-appropriate professional advice on business structure, contracts, invoicing, taxes, privacy and payment handling. This package does not determine legal obligations or complete registration.

No accounts were created, company registered, domain purchased, customer contacted or payment configured as part of this delivery.

## 10. Investor readiness later

An early pitch can explain problem, buyer, incident story, demonstration, alternatives, customer evidence, pricing experiment, team and next milestone. Do not invent market size, revenue or customer logos. A working demo plus documented paid usage is more useful now than a speculative large market slide. Build a data room only when fundraising is a real goal: incorporation/ownership records, financial model, contracts, product metrics and security posture.
